# Flora & Fauna Taxonomy

Reference document for all flora lineages, mixotrophic organisms, and fauna species. These are the named organisms of the planet — the vocabulary used in design, rendering, and game text. Include this alongside Ecology-Foundations.md and Design-Principles.md for any world-building, terrain, sprite, or creature work.

Avoid Earth language for these organisms. They are not trees, grass, mushrooms, wolves, or rabbits. Use the names defined here. Pattern-matching to Earth analogs erodes the alien quality of the world.

---

## The Three Lineages

All life on this planet belongs to one of three independent lineages:

**Photosynthetic flora** — microbial-descended producers that harvest light. The crimson surface of the planet. Three major clades: pela, kolm, veer.

**Chemotrophic flora** — microbial-descended producers that metabolize minerals. The mineral crusts, mounds, and streamers. Four major clades: grith, tosk, nath, krev.

**Animals** — two clades (A and B) descended from two Cambrian-level survivors. All consumers. Six baseline species described here: vaek, marek, skiv, brol, thrynn, keth.

The photosynthetic and chemotrophic lineages share a deep microbial ancestor but diverged roughly 350 MYA along energy-source lines. The animal clades are unrelated to either flora lineage.

## Photosynthetic Flora

All three photosynthetic clades share deep crimson-maroon photosynthetic pigment that absorbs blue, green, and violet light. Pigment color does not vary by mineral chemistry. What varies is structural tissue — trunks, holdfasts, and support material incorporate local minerals and take on their color (iron-red, copper-green, manganese-dark).

### Ancestral Divergence

The earliest major divergence was between edge-expanding organisms (sheet growth) and tip-growing organisms (directional/apical growth). This split occurred roughly 300 MYA.

The tip-growing branch later split again when one sub-lineage evolved mineral incorporation into structural tissue, enabling compression-resistant vertical growth. The other tip-growing lineage remained flexible and filamentous.

Result: three clades from two branching events.

### Pela (PEH-lah)

**Body plan:** Sheet-forming. Continuous tissue expanding outward from all edges simultaneously. No growing tip, no directional growth, no vertical structure. The ancestral growth form, closest to the microbial mat ancestor.

**Tissue structure:** Three-layered. Photosynthetic cells on top (the crimson surface). Structural polymer matrix in the middle (extracellular polymeric substances — biological glue). Absorptive cells on the bottom in contact with substrate, uptaking water and dissolved minerals. Thin internal channels distribute nutrients laterally — a proto-vascular system, but not capable of driving vertical transport.

**Growth forms:** Pela's form varies with conditions, but these are expressions of a single body plan, not separate species (though different pela species are adapted to different moisture regimes):

- *Crust*: 2-3mm thin film on rock or dry substrate. Dormancy-capable — surface cells desiccate and form a protective layer, deeper cells enter dormancy. Reactivates within hours when moisture returns. Minimal productivity. Anywhere there's intermittent moisture and light.

- *Mat*: 5-10mm+ continuous tissue layer on saturated ground. Fully hydrated, actively growing. The dominant ground cover of the planet. The crimson skin. Three internal layers at full development.

- *Cushion/hummock*: Mat building up on itself over time. Older dead material underneath, living surface on top. Raised 5-20cm. Common where water is adequate but conditions don't favor kolm establishment. The dead material beneath does not decompose quickly in saturated conditions — it compresses into a peat-like substrate.

- *Floating raft*: Submerged pela photosynthesizes and generates oxygen gas. Oxygen trapped in the structural middle layer creates buoyancy. Patches detach from substrate and float. In calm, sheltered, shallow water — lagoons, bays, lee sides of headlands — rafts can form extensive connected mats on the water surface. Absent from wave-exposed open water, fast currents, and deep water. Cropped by aquatic and semi-aquatic grazers (brol).

**Vertical limit:** ~15-20cm as a raised hummock of accumulated dead material. Pela cannot build a trunk or branch.

**Reproduction:** Spores released from specialized patches on the sheet surface at ground level. Wind, water splash, and animal disturbance disperse them. Also reproduces by fragmentation — any torn piece can establish a new colony on wet substrate. Fragmentation makes pela an aggressive colonizer. Physical disturbance creates propagules.

**Ecological role:** Pela is the fast colonizer. It arrives first on any newly available wet substrate. In areas with adequate moisture and mineral supply, pela establishes quickly and kolm eventually overtops it. Pela persists as ground cover under kolm canopy, photosynthesizing in deep shade at low efficiency. The ground cover in lowland forest is pela beneath kolm — two organisms coexisting, not one.

**Relationship to water:** Pela's microbial ancestor was aquatic. The mat form IS the aquatic form. "Terrestrial" pela is the derived condition. As water depth increases, pela doesn't die — it floats. The organism continuously bridges the land-water transition: thick mat on dry ground → wet mat in saturated ground → floating patches in shallow water → extensive rafts in calm deeper water → thinning and absence in wave-exposed open water.

**Viewer mapping:** groundCover in photosynthetic zones is almost entirely pela. The plains palette — warm red-brown ground with crimson accent — is pela mat mixed with dead pela detritus and exposed mineral substrate.

### Kolm (COLE-m)

**Body plan:** Mineral-ceramic structural builder. Apical (tip) growth. The lineage that evolved mineral incorporation into structural tissue, enabling compression-resistant vertical growth under gravity. The canopy organism.

**Tissue structure:** Four functional tissue types, the most differentiated of any photosynthetic organism on the planet:

- *Holdfast*: Spreading basal structure anchoring to substrate. Superficially resembles pela but structurally distinct — has apical growing tips at its advancing edges, not marginal expansion. Absorbs water and dissolved minerals across its entire contact surface. Not a deep taproot system — a broad, shallow, spreading anchor. Firmly attached in saturated fine substrate, poorly attached in coarse dry substrate.

- *Stele*: The structural core. A column of mineral-ceramic composite — living cells embedded in a matrix of precipitated mineral oxides (iron oxide, copper compound, manganese oxide, depending on local groundwater chemistry). Internal capillary channels wick water upward through the porous mineral-organic matrix. Transport mechanism is capillary wicking augmented by osmotic concentration gradients maintained by living cells — cells near the base are dilute, cells near the canopy concentrate sugars from photosynthesis and create osmotic pull. This is less efficient than Earth xylem and tops out at 3-8 meters, but it transpires much less water. New material deposited at the growing tip; older material lower down is essentially geological. The exterior surface shows layered, banded mineral-organic texture. Stele color reflects local mineral chemistry — iron-rust, copper-verdigris, manganese-dark. This is structural, not cosmetic: the color IS the building material.

- *Crown*: Branching framework at the stele top. Fractal branching pattern — species-specific. Some kolm species branch into broad flat domes. Others into asymmetric whorls. Others barely branch and have a single rosette. The branching transitions from single column to multiple radiating arms.

- *Fronds*: Broad, flat photosynthetic surfaces extending from crown branches. Thin mineral-polymer frame supporting a sheet of photosynthetic tissue (structurally simpler than the stele). The primary light-harvesting surfaces. Always deep crimson-maroon regardless of stele mineral coloration. Spore-producing structures develop on frond undersides. Elevated spore release (from 3-5m height) catches wind currents that ground-level pela spores cannot reach.

**Height:** Varies enormously by species, age, and mineral supply. Young kolm on poor substrate: 30cm. Typical well-established lowland kolm: 3-5m. Maximum on rich mineral substrate: ~8m. The low ceiling described in Ecology-Foundations — walking under kolm canopy feels like walking through a room with a dark low ceiling, not standing among towering columns.

**Growth rate:** Slow. Establishment from spore to visible structure: years. From visible structure to canopy height: decades. Kolm forests are old. Disturbance that removes kolm takes decades to recover. Pela fills the gap immediately.

**Reproduction:** Primarily spores, released from frond undersides. Kolm does NOT fragment effectively — a broken piece lacks holdfast cells needed to establish, and stele material is inert ceramic that can't regenerate.

**Trunk persistence:** The mineral-ceramic stele does not rot. It erodes geologically, not biologically. Dead kolm stands for years to decades as mineral pillars, slowly losing fronds, eventually crumbling. In flooded zones, dead kolm relicts stand in water long after conditions stopped favoring living kolm establishment. This produces the "flooded forest with standing dead trunks" visual in the wet zone.

**Relationship to water:** Existing kolm tolerates flooding at the base — the holdfast was established in saturated conditions and the stele is waterproof ceramic. Capillary transport continues. Canopy remains above water and photosynthesizes normally. But new kolm cannot establish in standing water deeper than a few centimeters because holdfast initiation requires stable substrate contact. In the wet zone transition (5-15cm standing water), existing kolm persists as relicts while no new kolm establishes. At 15-30cm standing water, existing kolm begins dying — the mineral supply to the stele is disrupted by the water column. Dead steles persist as structural relicts.

**Viewer mapping:** canopyDensity in photosynthetic zones is kolm. The forest palette — deep crimson canopy features, near-black floor — is kolm fronds overhead with pela mat in deep shade beneath. The structural_wood texture profile describes kolm stele surfaces.

### Veer (VEER)

**Body plan:** Filament-forming epiphyte. Apical (tip) growth, sister lineage to kolm, but never evolved mineral incorporation. Grows as fine, flexible, branching photosynthetic filaments. Individual filaments 0.5-2mm diameter. No rigid structural tissue. Cannot support its own weight vertically.

**Growth habit:** Grows on things. Drapes over kolm steles and crowns, hangs from crown frameworks, coats rock surfaces in wet areas, trails from cliff faces, dangles into streams from overhanging banks. In high-moisture environments, forms dense curtains of hanging filaments. In drier conditions, filaments mat together into a felt-like coating on sheltered rock faces.

**Niche:** Colonizes existing vertical and overhanging surfaces — cliff faces, kolm steles, cave mouths, undersides of rock overhangs. Pela covers horizontal surfaces. Kolm builds its own vertical surface. Veer colonizes surfaces that already exist. Anywhere there's moisture, some light, and a surface to cling to.

**Water transport:** Veer filaments wick water along their length through capillary action within the filament. A veer strand can hang 30cm below a wet rock overhang and remain hydrated at the tip.

**Cave transition:** Veer extends deepest into cave mouths of any photosynthetic organism. At cave entrances where ambient light penetrates, veer filaments grow on ceiling and walls, photosynthesizing in extremely low light. Veer is the transitional organism between photosynthetic surface ecology and chemotrophic underground ecology — in some cave systems, veer on walls near the entrance transitions to chemotrophic grith further in.

**Reproduction:** Spores from filament tips, wind-dispersed. Also fragments — broken filament pieces re-establish on suitable moist surfaces.

**Visual character:** Dark crimson threads and tangles. On kolm steles, veer reads as fuzzy or shaggy texture over the mineral surface — softening the geometric banding of the stele with organic filamentous growth. In dense stands, veer curtains look like hanging sheets of dark red filament. On rock faces, veer forms a velvety crimson coating.

**Viewer mapping:** Veer does not currently have a distinct representation in the viewer data model. It would manifest as a texture/detail modifier on kolm steles and rock surfaces in wet environments — visual complexity, not a separate flora type. At tile scale in the game, veer adds visual richness to forested and rocky tiles in high-moisture areas.

## Chemotrophic Flora

All chemotrophic organisms metabolize minerals for energy. They do not need light. Their color IS their chemistry — the organism takes on the color of whatever mineral it processes (manganese-violet, copper-teal, iron-rust). Color is a direct, honest signal of metabolic substrate.

A critical metabolic detail established in Underground-Chemotrophic-Ecology.md: the dominant chemotrophic lineage evolved a pathway that **splits water as part of mineral-processing chemistry**, releasing O₂ as a metabolic byproduct. Wherever chemotrophic flora grows densely, it produces breathable oxygen. This applies both underground AND on the surface.

### Ancestral Divergence

The earliest major divergence was between organisms that grew ON substrate surfaces and organisms that grew INTO them (endolithic borers). This split occurred roughly 300 MYA.

The surface-growing branch then diversified along geometric axes: sheet-formers (maximum surface contact), accretionary builders (three-dimensional mineral-processing volume), and filament-formers (maximum contact with dissolved minerals in flowing water).

Result: four clades from two branching events.

### Grith (GRITH, rhymes with "pith")

**Body plan:** Sheet-forming surface crust. The chemotrophic equivalent of pela. Continuous films and crusts coating rock surfaces, processing whatever mineral substrate they sit on. The ancestral surface growth form — the simplest, most ubiquitous chemotrophic organism.

**Tissue structure:** Mineralized and slightly glossy. Grith precipitates a thin mineral layer on its upper surface as a metabolic byproduct, creating a protective crust. The active cells live beneath this mineral cap. Old grith surfaces are essentially thin layers of mineral rock with living cells underneath.

**Texture:** Geological rather than biological. Fine concentric growth arcs radiating from expansion centers. Ridge lines where two spreading grith sheets have met and can't interdigitate (competitive boundaries). The three-layer color doc's chemotrophic_mat texture profile — "near-uniform mineral crust with concentric growth arcs and ridge lines" — describes grith.

**Growth rate:** Slow. Centimeters per year on typical substrate. Cannot outrun pela for colonizing open ground. Wins by persistence: tolerates conditions pela cannot (no light, extreme mineral concentrations, very low water on bare rock).

**Ecological role — soil builder:** Dead grith does not decompose the way dead pela does. Dead pela breaks down into amber-brown organic detritus. Dead grith leaves behind a mineral crust — precipitated oxide on the rock surface. Over geological time, these crusts weather, fragment, and mix with dead organic material to produce the fine-grained mineral-organic substrate that pela and kolm grow on. Grith creates soil. On young volcanic islands with fresh lava, grith is among the first colonizers — coats rock, processes surface minerals, builds weathering crust, eventually creates conditions for pela and kolm establishment.

**Shade competition:** Under kolm canopy where light is too dim for pela to be productive, grith can slowly colonize the ground surface because it does not need light. In deep shade, grith outcompetes pela by default.

**Viewer mapping:** The fungal_grass palette — dark violet mineral crust in manganese zones — is grith. The "chemotrophic ground mat" in the regional overlay. Underground, grith is the baseline chemotrophic organism on every wet rock surface.

### Tosk (TOSK, rhymes loosely with "dusk")

**Body plan:** Accretionary builder. Domed, layered structures built up in concentric mineral-encrusted layers over time. The chemotrophic equivalent of kolm — the lineage that evolved three-dimensional structural building. But where kolm builds upward to reach light, tosk builds outward to maximize mineral-processing volume.

**Growth mechanism:** Directed accretion. Instead of spreading as a thin film (grith), tosk adds new layers on TOP of old layers. Each new layer is a living surface of mineral-processing cells atop older, now-inert mineralized layers. A cross-section shows concentric rings — each ring a former living surface, now mineralized. The organism builds geological structure as a metabolic consequence.

**Why mounds work:** A flat grith film processes only the mineral directly beneath it. Once that surface layer is oxidized, growth slows. Tosk's hemispherical surface area exceeds its substrate footprint — a 30cm diameter mound has ~1400 cm² of active surface over ~700 cm² of footprint. Double the mineral access without doubling territory.

**Energy reserves:** Old tosk mounds accumulate partially-processed mineral residue in their inert interior layers. Secondary metabolic reactions continue slowly inside old mounds — extracting remaining energy from incompletely oxidized minerals. An old tosk mound is a slow-burning energy reserve. This is why tosk concentrates around seeps: it establishes where mineral delivery is continuous, and its accumulated body mass stores energy for lean periods.

**Three structural expressions, one organism:**

- *Mound*: The default form on horizontal surfaces. Domed, concentrically layered. Surface tosk: 30-50cm diameter typical, exceptional specimens up to 1m. Underground in deep seep zones: potentially much larger (centuries of growth on rich substrate).

- *Bracket*: Tosk on vertical surfaces. Same accretionary growth, but gravity constrains geometry. Layers deposited on a vertical face spread sideways and downward, producing horizontal shelf structures projecting from walls. A tosk colony on a surface transitioning from horizontal to vertical shows mound on the floor grading into bracket on the wall. Same organism, continuous growth, different geometry.

- *Spire*: Reproductive organ. When a tosk colony reaches maturity, it produces thin vertical stalks from the mound surface — the growing tip concentrating energy into rapid upward extension. Spire cap develops sporangia releasing spores. Underground, spires orient toward air drafts (growing tip responds to airflow as directional cue). Presence of spires indicates a mature, well-fed colony. 1px wide at 16×16 scale, 6-8px tall. Sparse — at most 1-2 per tile on mature colonies.

**Reproduction:** Primarily spores from spire tips. Also slow edge expansion of mound base. Spore dispersal is wind-driven (surface) or draft-driven (underground).

**Viewer mapping:** The mushroom and mushforest terrain types are tosk zones. The colony mound sprite — wide dome silhouette with horizontal growth banding — is tosk. The chemotrophic_colony texture profile describes tosk surfaces. Bracket shelving is tosk on walls. Spires are tosk reproductive organs.

### Nath (NATH, rhymes with "math")

**Body plan:** Filament-forming. Branching filamentous structures anchored to rock in flowing water. The chemotrophic equivalent of veer — maximum surface area — but for dissolved mineral contact rather than light interception.

**Growth habit:** Anchors to rock surfaces in streams, channels, and seeps. Extends branching filaments into the water column. Filaments 1-3mm diameter, branching repeatedly into dense tangled masses. Each filament surface is chemotrophically active, processing dissolved minerals from flowing water. Dense nath growth creates turbulence that slows flow and increases mineral contact time.

**Metabolic interface:** Nath is the only chemotrophic lineage that primarily processes DISSOLVED minerals rather than solid substrate. Grith and tosk sit on rock and process the mineral in the rock. Nath sits in water and processes minerals dissolved in the water. Dissolved minerals are continuously replenished by flowing water — effectively unlimited supply as long as flow continues. This makes nath growth rate highly responsive to conditions: rich mineral water produces explosive growth, loss of mineral supply produces rapid die-back.

**Aquatic dependency:** Nath needs flowing water for mineral delivery and physical support. Filaments aren't rigid enough to hold shape in air. Out of water, nath collapses and desiccates. Distribution is tightly coupled to hydrology: wherever there's mineral-rich flowing water, there's nath. Where streams dry up, nath dies. Seasonal flow changes produce boom-bust populations.

**Reproduction:** Spores from filament tips, water-dispersed (not wind-dispersed). Also fragments carried by current. Water-dispersal constraint means nath populations follow drainage networks. An isolated pool with no stream connection has no nath. A connected stream system has nath throughout.

**Viewer mapping:** Nath does not currently have a distinct representation in the viewer. At tile scale, nath would appear as colored filamentous texture in stream and channel tiles — visual detail within water terrain, not a separate flora type in the data model. Its color follows local mineral chemistry.

### Krev (KREV, rhymes with "rev")

**Body plan:** Endolithic borer. Grows INTO rock rather than ON it. The only chemotrophic lineage with no photosynthetic parallel — no photosynthetic organism has reason to bore into opaque rock.

**Growth mechanism:** The active growth front is a cell layer millimeters thick at the rock-organism interface. Secretes acids and chelating compounds to dissolve the mineral matrix ahead of it. Behind the growth front, dissolved space fills with mucilaginous organic matrix maintaining moisture and chemical conditions. Advances at millimeters to centimeters per year depending on rock hardness and mineral richness.

**Visibility:** Mostly invisible. What's visible on colonized rock surfaces: entry pores (small holes, 1-5mm diameter), sometimes with thin mineral-colored waste crusts around them. In cross-section, krev-colonized rock is perforated with fine channel networks radiating from entry pores, filled with organic matrix and active growth fronts at advancing tips.

**Ecological role — geological force:** Krev is the primary agent of biological rock weathering on the planet. Fresh volcanic rock gets colonized within years of cooling. Boring weakens rock structure. Physical weathering breaks krev-weakened rock into fragments. Fragments become raw material for soil.

**Succession role:** Krev and grith work together in the weathering sequence on fresh volcanic surfaces. Krev perforates and weakens fresh rock from within. Grith crusts the surface and processes exposed minerals. Weathered fragments accumulate as coarse substrate. Pela colonizes accumulated fines. Given enough time and moisture, kolm establishes. The ecological succession on fresh volcanic substrate: krev → grith → pela → kolm.

**Dead zone persistence:** Underground, in dead zones where surface minerals are exhausted, krev accesses unoxidized minerals deeper in the rock matrix. Krev colonies in dead zones are slow and energy-limited but persistent. Over geological time, they open rock to deeper oxidation, potentially reactivating dead zones.

**Reproduction:** Spores released from entry pores, carried by water or air to new rock surfaces. Mineral-generalist — different krev species specialize in different rock chemistries. Cannot bore into fully oxidized rock (no energy to drive dissolution).

**Viewer mapping:** Krev is invisible at current viewer scales. Its contribution is indirect — the soil that pela and kolm grow on exists because krev (and grith) broke down the parent rock. Krev is geological infrastructure.

## Mixotrophic Organisms

Mixotrophy is NOT a lineage. It is a metabolic capability that evolved convergently from multiple ancestors. On this planet, the photosynthetic and chemotrophic lineages share deep ancestral biochemistry — the genetic distance for horizontal gene transfer between them is small. Mixotrophy arises repeatedly, from multiple directions.

A "mixotrophic zone" in the viewer is an area where dual-energy metabolism is competitively favored (both light AND mineral substrate available). The organisms that fill this zone come from multiple ancestral lineages, all expressing dual metabolism differently. The zone is inherently mixed — not one uniform organism, but a patchwork of mixo-capable descendants of different parent lineages.

### Solk (SOLK, rhymes with "folk")

**Ancestor:** Pela (photosynthetic sheet-former).

**What happened:** A pela lineage near mineral seeps acquired chemotrophic genes. The absorptive bottom layer became chemically active — metabolizing dissolved minerals for energy in addition to photosynthesizing on the top surface.

**Body plan:** Still a flat sheet (pela architecture). Top surface photosynthetic (crimson). Bottom surface chemotrophic (processing dissolved minerals from substrate water). A metabolic sandwich.

**Niche:** Margins of mineral seeps, mineral-enriched stream banks — zones where pure pela would struggle because mineral concentration is toxic. Solk metabolizes the excess, turning toxic mineral load into energy source. Double energy input (light + minerals) gives competitive advantage over either specialist alone.

**Color:** Crimson top surface tinted by chemotrophic byproducts leaching upward from the base layer. In manganese zones: red-purple. In copper zones: dark muted mauve. In iron zones: deep warm red (barely distinguishable from pure pela). The three-layer color doc's mixotrophic tissue colors are solk colors.

**Abundance:** Probably the most common mixotrophic organism because pela is the most common photosynthetic organism and the metabolic addition is simple.

---

### Tharn (THARN, one syllable, rhymes with "barn")

**Ancestor:** Tosk (chemotrophic accretionary builder).

**What happened:** A tosk lineage near the surface acquired photosynthetic pigment on its upper growing surface. The uppermost growth layer has photosynthetic cells interspersed with mineral-processing cells.

**Body plan:** Small colony mound (tosk architecture). 10-30cm diameter, 5-15cm tall. Mineral-banded sides (chemotrophic body). Distinctly crimson crown (photosynthetic upper surface). The contrast between mineral body and photosynthetic cap makes tharn visually distinctive — "half rock, half plant."

**Niche:** Marginal zones where neither photosynthetic nor chemotrophic specialists reach full productivity. Both energy sources available but neither alone is rich enough for large specialists. Tharn is a generalist.

**Light sensitivity:** In strong light near the surface, photosynthetic contribution is significant. In low light or near cave mouths, photosynthetic contribution becomes minimal and the organism is functionally chemotrophic.

**Sprite profile:** At 16×16 tile scale: small dome (4-6px wide, 3-4px tall) with horizontal growth banding on sides, fuzzy red top. Distinct from both pure tosk mounds (larger, fully mineral-colored) and from pela hummocks (solid crimson, no mineral banding).

---

### Dren (DREN, one syllable, rhymes with "wren")

**Ancestor:** Veer (photosynthetic filament-former).

**What happened:** A veer lineage colonized mineral-rich rock surfaces and evolved the ability to extract energy from substrate minerals it clings to. Filaments are photosynthetic on outer surfaces and chemotrophically active where they contact mineral substrate.

**Niche — cave transition:** Dren is the organism found at cave mouths. Near the entrance, dren filaments photosynthesize AND process minerals from the rock. Deeper in, where light drops below viability, dren runs entirely on mineral energy. Some deep-cave dren populations have lost photosynthetic pigment entirely — secondarily chemotrophic.

**Niche — kolm parasite:** Dren grows on kolm steles near mineral seeps. A kolm stele is a mineral-ceramic structure. Veer normally grows on it epiphytically, using it as physical support. Dren additionally metabolizes minerals in the kolm stele's ceramic matrix — slowly dissolving the mineral structure. Over decades, dren-colonized kolm steles become slightly pitted and weakened. Not enough to kill the kolm. Enough to produce a distinctive visual texture: crimson-tinted filamentous growth with visible pitting on the stele surface beneath.

**Color:** Like veer but mineral-tinted. Dark red-purple in manganese zones. Dark muted filaments in copper zones. Same hanging, tangled, velvety texture as veer, shifted off pure crimson toward local mineral chemistry.

### What a Mixotrophic Zone Looks Like at Tile Scale

The viewer says "this cell is mixotrophic." The player sees a patchwork, not one organism:

- **Solk mats** covering the ground — pela-like sheets tinted by local mineral chemistry. Red-purple, muted mauve, or deep warm red depending on mineral regime. The continuous ground cover, but off-crimson.
- **Tharn mounds** scattered across the solk mat — small mineral domes with crimson caps. Visually distinct from both pure tosk (larger, fully mineral-colored) and pela cushions (solid crimson).
- **Kolm steles colonized by dren** — mineral-tinted filamentous growth on trunks, with visible pitting. Canopy fronds remain pure crimson (dren grows on steles, not fronds).
- **Patches of pure pela and pure grith** interdigitating at the zone boundaries — the transition is a gradient, not a line.

## Fauna

Six baseline surface species. These are common, generalist-niche organisms — the first fauna the player encounters. Weird specialists, deep-water oddities, and colonial superorganisms come later.

Full body maps, transducer layouts, pathway topologies, and derived values are in Surface-Creatures.md. This section provides the name vocabulary and quick reference only.

### Vaek (VAEK, rhymes with "lake")

**Clade A meso-predator.** The common surface predator. Dog-sized, six limbs, chemical-dominant sensing. Tracks by scent trail. Readable state machine — ear-flaps telegraph attention. Pack-optional with personality variance (pack-tendency, lone hunter, wary). The first dangerous thing the player learns to deal with.

Code reference: `wolf` → rename to `vaek`

### Marek (MAR-ek, two syllables)

**Clade A apex predator.** The large, dangerous version of the vaek. Same lineage, different scale. Solitary, longer detection range, more experienced. The player's vaek instincts transfer but the margin for error disappears. Detects the player well before the player detects it.

Code reference: `dire_wolf` → rename to `marek`

### Skiv (SKIV, rhymes with "give")

**Clade B small herbivore.** Everywhere. Harmless. Fast. Eight limbs — front four grazing independently, rear four locomotory. The first Clade B creature the player encounters. Introduces the "doing several things at once" feel of Clade B. Vibration-dominant sensing. Flees when approached.

Code reference: `hare` → rename to `skiv`

### Brol (BROL, rhymes with "roll")

**Clade A large herbivore.** The big amphibious grazer. Hippo-manatee scale. Six limbs, front pair paddle-like. Crosses land and water freely. Not aggressive but tanky. Chemical sensing adapted for aquatic conditions — reads dissolved minerals. Makes coastlines and shallows feel inhabited. Not worth fighting.

Code reference: `cave_crab` → rename to `brol`

### Thrynn (THRYNN, one syllable, rhymes with "thin")

**Clade B colonial chemotroph.** The swarm. Individual nodes are passive. Collectively dangerous. Enzymatic contact digestion — the node's surface is an ongoing chemical reaction. Damage scales with number of adjacent nodes. Detection via inter-node vibrational drumming. The hum IS the colony thinking. Louder = more attention allocated to the player.

"Thrynn" works as both singular (one node) and collective ("the thrynn are converging"). No separate colony name needed.

Code reference: `mushroom` (creature) → rename to `thrynn`

### Keth (KETH, one syllable, rhymes with "death")

**Clade B solitary ambush predator.** Territory-bound, vibration-sensing, explosive ambush. Armored — thickened, stiffened integument. Compact, low, crouching posture. Front sensor limbs read ground vibration with extreme precision. Rear limbs built for explosive short bursts. No readable behavioral states. Strikes and withdraws in the same action. Lethal in home territory, cautious and ineffective outside it.

Code reference: (no current code entry) → `keth`

## Ecological Succession Reference

On a fresh volcanic surface, colonization follows the mineral-to-soil pipeline:

1. **Krev** bores into cooling rock, accessing reduced minerals within
2. **Grith** crusts the rock surface, processing exposed minerals
3. Physical weathering breaks krev-weakened, grith-crusted rock into fragments
4. Fragments accumulate as coarse mineral-organic substrate
5. **Pela** colonizes accumulated fines where moisture is adequate
6. **Kolm** establishes on pela-covered substrate where mineral-water supply sustains stele building (decades)
7. **Veer** colonizes established kolm steles and wet rock surfaces
8. Animal colonization follows flora establishment (food base required)

In mineral-rich zones, tosk and nath establish on the grith-crusted surfaces and in streams. In transition zones where both light and mineral energy are available, mixotrophic organisms (solk, tharn, dren) fill the ecotone.

## What This Document Constrains

| Decision | Constrained by |
|---|---|
| Ground cover visual | Pela (photosynthetic) or grith (chemotrophic) or solk (mixotrophic) — three distinct organisms, three distinct textures and colors |
| Canopy structures | Kolm only (photosynthetic). Tosk mounds are NOT canopy — they are ground-level accretionary structures. No "chemotrophic canopy" exists. |
| Trunk/stele color | Mineral chemistry of local groundwater incorporated into kolm stele ceramic. Iron-rust, copper-verdigris, manganese-dark. Color IS structure. |
| Frond/mat color | Photosynthetic pigment — always crimson-maroon regardless of mineral chemistry. Does not vary. |
| Chemotrophic organism color | Mineral chemistry of substrate being metabolized. Color IS chemistry. Manganese-violet, copper-teal, iron-rust. |
| Mixotrophic zone visual | Patchwork of solk (tinted pela), tharn (small mounds with red caps), dren (tinted veer), plus interdigitating pure pela and grith patches. NOT a uniform third color. |
| Water-flora transition | Pela continuously bridges land to water: mat → floating raft. Kolm persists as structural relicts in flooded zones. No hard flora boundary at the waterline. |
| Underground flora | Same chemotrophic lineages (grith, tosk, nath, krev) as surface, unconstrained by light competition, diversified into full producer role. |
| Ecological succession | Krev → grith → pela → kolm. Mineral weathering produces soil that photosynthetic organisms colonize. |
| Fauna naming | Vaek, marek, skiv, brol, thrynn, keth. No Earth animal names in game text. |
| Sprite design | Kolm stele is NOT a tree trunk. Tosk mound is NOT a mushroom. Pela mat is NOT grass. Design from organism anatomy, not Earth parallels. |
